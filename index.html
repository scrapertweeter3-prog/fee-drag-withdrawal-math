Same 6 percent, $630,429 apart

Two retirees hold the same $600,000 in stock funds earning 6 percent gross and withdraw $24,000 a year. One owns a plain index fund with a 0.05 percent expense ratio and ends at $1,516,881 after 30 years. The other pays a 1 percent adviser wrap on top of a 0.25 percent fund, nets 4.75 percent, and ends at $886,452. Both saw the same markets. The difference is $630,429, which is 71 percent of everything the wrapped lane has left.

The wrap never shows up as a bill. The first-year wrap is $6,000, and against a $24,000 withdrawal the fee is a quarter of the money you drew. The fund's own 0.25 percent adds another $1,500 that year. No invoice arrives for the $6,000 because the wrap is deducted inside the fund before statements are cut, so the account shows a 6 percent gross and quietly compounds at 4.75 percent. Over 30 years the adviser collects $211,885 in wrap fees, while the gap between the two endings reaches $630,429.

The fix costs nothing and reads like nothing: one transfer out of the wrapped share class into the 0.05 percent index. The adviser's real work, asset location, tax-loss harvesting, rebalancing, is worth some share of that 1 percent. Price it against the ledger: a comparable wrap-free fund family charges 0.15 percent all-in for the same work, 0.15 percent instead of 1.25 percent. At the 4 percent safe-withdrawal framing, every $1 of annual fee removes $25 of portfolio capacity, so a $6,000 wrap must add $150,000 a year of value to break even. Fees are the one input in the ledger you set by paperwork alone.
